What the evidence shows

Zhao et al.’s JMIR systematic review lists decreased waiting time among the recurring benefits after clinics adopt web-based scheduling (reported for 6 of 21 systems reviewed). One detailed example in that review is Cao and colleagues’ hospital Web-based Appointment System in China, where average total waiting time fell from 98 minutes to 7 minutes because patients no longer had to queue physically to obtain an appointment. Another UK sexual-health eTriage program raised the share of patients offered an appointment within the required 48-hour window from 48% to 100%.

  • Waiting-time improvement cited for 6/21 systems (Zhao et al., JMIR 2017).
  • Chinese hospital WAS: average wait 98 min → 7 min (Cao et al., cited in JMIR 2017).
  • UK eTriage: appointments offered within 48 h rose from 48% → 100% (cited in JMIR 2017).

Faster access through self-rescheduling

Beyond the initial booking queue, digital tools can shorten the wait until the actual visit. A 2024 JMIR retrospective cohort on an EHR-based Fast Pass self-rescheduling tool reported that patients who accepted earlier openings improved their appointment timing by a median of 14 days. That is not “magic marketing speed” — it is unused earlier capacity being offered back to patients who want it.

  • Fast Pass acceptors: median 14-day earlier appointment (Gallo et al., JMIR 2024).
  • Real-time systems make open slots transparent so patients claim them without phone backlog (JMIR 2017).

Why waits shrink

Phone booking creates two waits: waiting on hold for a scheduler, then waiting for an available clinical slot. Real-time online calendars remove the scheduler queue for simple bookings. Same-day or soon slots, when clinics enable them, can further compress request-to-visit time. The JMIR review also notes that late cancellations can be reused more quickly online than through slow phone turnaround — recovering capacity that would otherwise sit empty.
